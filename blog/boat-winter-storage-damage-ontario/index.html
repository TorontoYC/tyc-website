What We Find in April

The first warm week of April is the busiest stretch of our year, and it is also the most informative one. Wraps come off, covers come off, and for about ten days we are looking at what six months of an Ontario winter did to a few hundred boats that were all put away at roughly the same time, by owners who all thought they had done it right.

Most of them had. But the ones that come out with a problem almost never have a weather problem. They have an October problem. Something small got skipped in the two hours before the boat went under cover, and it had six months in the dark to turn into a real number.

Here is what we actually see, in roughly the order it shows up.

The smell before anything else

You know within a second of getting a corner of the wrap up. A boat that breathed all winter smells like cold plastic and nothing else. A boat that did not smells like a basement.

That is condensation, and it is the single most common thing we find. A boat is a big cold object full of soft materials, sitting under a sealed sheet. Every mild day in February pushes warm air into that space, every cold night drops it back below the dew point, and the water that comes out of the air goes into the headliner, the cushions, the carpet backing and the underside of the deck. Nothing leaked. The water was already inside.

The fix costs nothing in October and a lot in April. Vents in the wrap, high and low so air actually moves rather than just having a hole. Cushions up on edge or out of the boat entirely. Lockers and the fridge left open. A drying agent in the cabin if the boat is closed up tight. Owners who do all four almost never have a mildew conversation with us.

The ones who do are looking at an interior deep clean, and if it got into the headliner or the backing of the carpet it is not always a cleaning job at all. We covered where cleaning stops and repair starts, and mildew is one of the places that line gets crossed without anybody deciding to cross it.

The stripe down the hull

This one is a tarp problem, and it is the reason we keep saying the same thing about covers.

A tarp on a boat for six months does not sit still. It moves every time the wind blows, and it is dragging whatever grit is under it back and forth across the same eight inches of gelcoat, thousands of times, in the dark, all winter. What you get in the spring is a dull band running the length of the hull, sometimes with the rope pattern visible in it. It polishes out if it is shallow. If the tarp had a grommet or a metal ring resting against the hull, it does not polish out, and you are into gel coat repair on a boat that was fine in November.

The related version is a tarp that pooled. Water sits in a low spot, freezes, gets heavier, pulls the whole cover down onto the arch or the windshield frame, and something bends. We see two or three of those a year and it is always the same story: it was a good tarp, and it was fine in December.

We wrote out the full comparison of shrink wrap against tarps if you want the detail. The short version is that the framing under the cover matters more than the material over it.

What was left on the hull in October

A boat put away dirty comes out worse than dirty.

Waterline scum, bird mess and sunscreen are all mildly acidic or mildly alkaline, and sitting against gelcoat for six months at low temperature with no UV and no rinse, they etch. In September that whole band comes off with soap, a brush and an afternoon. In April the same band needs a machine and a compound, which is a different service at a different price, and if it went deep enough the gloss does not fully come back.

This is the cheapest maintenance decision available to a boat owner and it is the one most commonly skipped, because in October the boat is going away and it feels pointless to clean something nobody will look at until spring. It is the opposite. A wash before the boat goes under cover is worth more than a wash after it comes out.

Bird mess is the worst offender and it is the one people find surprising. It will leave a visible ghost in the gelcoat after one winter.

Water that was still in something

Ontario gets cold enough, for long enough, that anything holding water and not drained is a coin flip.

The obvious ones get caught. Engine blocks and outdrives are usually handled by whoever winterizes the boat, and that work is generally done properly because everybody understands the consequence. What gets missed is everything else: a raw water washdown pump, a shower sump, a freshwater tank that was left half full, an ice maker, a livewell, a bilge with two inches in it because the drain plug went back in before the boat left the yard.

The drain plug is the one that comes up every single spring. It is a thirty second check that gets skipped because the boat is on a trailer in a hurry, and a bilge full of ice is not a small problem.

Fuel, batteries and mice

Three quick ones that account for a lot of April phone calls.

Fuel with ethanol in it pulls moisture out of the air, and a half-empty tank has a lot of air in it. Over a winter that water can separate out and sit at the bottom of the tank, which is exactly where the pickup is. Full tank, stabilizer, done.

Batteries left connected all winter drain slowly through whatever is still drawing, then freeze, and a frozen lead acid battery is finished. Disconnected and out of the boat if you can, or on a maintainer if it stays.

Mice get in through gaps you would swear were sealed and they go for wiring insulation, foam and anything soft. A boat with a nest in it in April is not usually a cleaning job, it is a wiring inspection. Steel wool in the obvious openings and no food left aboard covers most of it.

What all of this costs

The pattern across every one of these is the same and it is worth saying plainly. Prevention is measured in an afternoon and a few hundred dollars. The repair is measured in days and thousands.

An interior that grew mildew because the wrap had no vents is a deep clean at best and new headliner or carpet at worst. A hull with a chafe band from a flogging tarp is a compound job, and that is refinishing work, not detailing. Gelcoat etched by a season of scum may never come all the way back. A cracked block, a frozen battery and a tank of separated fuel are all real money for something that was avoidable in an afternoon.

None of this is a reason to panic in October. It is a reason to spend that afternoon.

October is when this gets decided

Every problem in this post was created before the boat went under cover, and none of them could be fixed after. If you take one thing from it, take the order of operations: clean the hull, drain everything that holds water, deal with fuel and batteries, then cover it, and make sure the cover has real framing and real venting rather than just being tight.
