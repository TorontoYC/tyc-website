Shrink Wrap vs Tarp vs Indoor Storage: What Actually Protects a Boat Over an Ontario Winter

Cold does not damage boats. Water does.

A hull sitting in a dry, freezing yard from November to April is fine. The damage comes from water that finds its way in, sits somewhere it should not, freezes, expands, thaws, and does it again the next week. Ontario gives you dozens of those cycles a winter. That is the thing your winter cover is actually fighting.

So the question is not "covered or uncovered." It is which cover keeps water out for five months without anybody standing over it.

The three real options

A tarp over a support frame. Cheapest, reusable, and genuinely fine on some boats.

Shrink wrap. Heat-shrunk plastic pulled tight to the hull over a frame, vented, and cut off in the spring. Redone every year.

Indoor storage. The best protection available and the most expensive, with limited spots at most marinas in this area.

Most boats on Lake Simcoe and Georgian Bay end up in one of the first two, so that is where the real decision sits.

Where a tarp wins, and where it fails

A well-built tarp setup works. If you have a proper frame with enough pitch, a tarp in good condition, and you are the kind of owner who goes and looks at the boat in February, a tarp will get you through the winter.

Tarps fail in a specific and predictable way. Snow lands on a section that does not have enough slope. It sits, it compresses, it melts a little in the afternoon and refreezes overnight. Now you have a pocket holding water and ice, and that weight pulls the tarp down further, which makes the pocket bigger. Eventually the pocket wins. It either tears the tarp, lifts an edge, or dumps the whole load into the cockpit.

The other tarp problem is movement. Loose tarp edges flap all winter, and vinyl or poly rubbing against gel coat for five months in the wind leaves marks that need to be buffed out in the spring.

Both of those are frame problems more than tarp problems. A tarp thrown over the boat and bungeed down is not a winter cover. A tarp over a real frame with steep sides is.

Where shrink wrap wins

Shrink wrap solves the snow load problem by geometry. It is pulled tight over a peaked frame, so there is no slack for snow to collect in and nothing loose to flap. Snow slides off or blows off. The film is heat-sealed to itself rather than tied, so there are no edges to lift in a January wind.

It also seals better than anything you can rig with grommets and rope, which matters more than most people expect. Rodents looking for somewhere warm find gaps in a tarp setup. A wrapped boat with the vents installed properly is a much harder target.

The trade-offs are real and worth saying plainly. Shrink wrap is a recurring cost every single year, it goes in the garbage each spring unless you have somewhere to recycle it, and once it is on you cannot get into the boat without cutting it.

The failure everybody gets wrong: moisture from the inside

Here is the one that actually causes damage, and it happens to wrapped boats more than tarped ones.

Shrink wrap does not breathe. If the boat goes under the wrap with water in the bilge, damp cushions, a wet carpet, or a canvas top folded up in the cabin, all of that moisture is now sealed in with the boat for five months. It evaporates, hits the cold plastic, condenses, and rains back down on the interior. You open it in April to mildew on every soft surface.

The wrap did not cause that. The boat was put away wet.

What prevents it:

  • Vents in the wrap. Not one token vent, enough for the size of the boat, positioned to move air front to back.
  • Bilge dry and the drain plug out.
  • Cushions up on edge, or better, out of the boat and stored somewhere dry.
  • Nothing damp left inside. No wet towels, no life jackets that went in soaked, no canvas.
  • The interior cleaned out before it is covered. Crumbs and food are what invite rodents in the first place.

The same rules apply under a tarp. A tarp is just more forgiving because it leaks a little air by design.

Take the canvas off

Your bimini, enclosure, or mooring cover is built for sun and rain in July. It is not built to hold a snow load or sit under a wrap for five months, and leaving it up is one of the fastest ways to shorten its life. Stitching, zippers, and clear vinyl windows all suffer in the cold, and vinyl windows can crack outright if they get folded while stiff.

Take it down, let it dry completely, and store it loosely rolled somewhere that is not the boat. If a seam or a zipper is already going, the off-season is the right time to get it fixed rather than discovering it on the first warm weekend in May. We do canvas and upholstery work at Keswick and Lagoon City through the winter.

If you are not sure which cover you actually need for next season, we wrote a separate guide on bimini versus full enclosure versus mooring cover.

So which one should you do?

A tarp is enough if: the boat is older, the gel coat is not something you are protecting, you have a proper frame with steep sides, and you will physically check on it during the winter.

Shrink wrap makes sense if: the boat is newer or you care about the finish, it stores somewhere you will not visit for months, it is an open bow or a boat with a lot of exposed upholstery, or you have ever pulled a cover off in the spring and found standing water.

Indoor storage makes sense if you can get a spot and the number works for you. It is the best answer. It is also the one most people on Simcoe and Georgian Bay cannot get.

There is no wrong answer here, only a mismatch between what the boat is worth to you and what you spent protecting it.

One thing about timing

The date that matters is not when you book. It is when your boat physically comes out of the water.

Haul-out is controlled by your marina, not by you and not by us. A boat that comes out in mid October gets wrapped in good weather on a normal schedule. A boat that comes out in December gets wrapped in the cold, at the back of the line, in the narrowest part of the season.

If you know you want the boat wrapped this year, the useful phone call is the one to your marina about a haul-out date, and it is worth making it earlier than feels necessary.
